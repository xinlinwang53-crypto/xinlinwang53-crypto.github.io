01 / Section 16.1

Ask the neighbors.

Keep the training examples. For a new input, find its K nearest neighbors and count their labels. This is an exemplar-based, nonparametric model: its stored representation can grow with the dataset.

p(y = c | x, D) = (1/K) ∑n ∈ Nₖ(x,D) 𝟙(yₙ = c)

Example: if 3 of the 5 nearest examples are blue, p(blue | x, D) = 3/5 = 0.6. Predict the majority class. Choose K and the distance metric using validation data; scale features when their units differ.

K controls flexibility

Small K: flexible, noisy boundaries; low bias and high variance. Large K: smoother boundaries; more bias and less variance.

1-NN creates Voronoi cells and usually fits the training set exactly when each point can be its own neighbor and labels are unambiguous. That does not imply good test accuracy.

Dimensions dilute locality

To capture a fraction p of a uniform D-dimensional unit cube, a neighborhood cube needs side length p1/D.

For p = 0.10. In 10D, side length ≈ 0.794: “nearby” spans most of each coordinate range. Use useful features, learned metrics, or stronger modeling assumptions.

Memory and search cost

Store examples: roughly O(ND) memory. A brute-force query computes N distances: O(ND), before neighbor selection.

Prune unnecessary examples or use approximate nearest neighbors. The reading mentions k-d trees, clustering, locality-sensitive hashing and FAISS. Exact search becomes difficult in high dimensions.

A theoretical result, not a finite-data promise: under standard conditions, asymptotic 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error. This does not say every KNN model is Bayes-optimal or that convergence is fast.

When the next face is someone new

Closed set: choose among known identities. Open set: allow “unknown.” Compare the query with a gallery; a calibrated similarity threshold can reject weak matches.

Novelty detection: recognize an unseen category, such as a new person. OOD detection: flag input outside the expected distribution, such as a dog photo sent to a face system. These can overlap.

Online / continual learning: obtain a label and add the new example to the gallery. Few-shot recognition: work with very few labeled examples per class. Related open-world tasks include entity resolution and multi-object tracking.

Adding gallery entries need not retrain the encoder. A nearest neighbor alone cannot determine whether something is unknown; rejection needs a rule. Novelty versus OOD is not always separable using distance alone.

02 / Section 16.2.2

First, learn the map.

Two photos of the same person may differ in lighting and pose. Pixel distance can be large even though identity is unchanged. An embedding keeps the aspects relevant to your task.

Input → encoder → embedding

e = f(x; θ) ∈ ℝᴸ
ê = e / ‖e‖₂

A neural network learns θ. Normalizing nonzero embeddings puts them on the unit hypersphere. Compare the resulting vectors instead of raw inputs.

L can be much smaller than the input dimension. Dimension reduction helps only if useful information is preserved.

Two views of the same geometry

Cosine similarity: s = êᵢᵀêⱼ
Squared Euclidean distance: q = ‖êᵢ − êⱼ‖₂²
q = 2 − 2s

Larger s means more similar; smaller q means more similar. Unit vectors have s ∈ [−1, 1] and q ∈ [0, 4]. Their nearest-neighbor rankings agree.

What supervises the map? Class labels can define positive pairs (same class) and negative pairs (different classes). Pair relationships can also be supplied without class identities, or constructed through suitable self-supervised augmentations. “Similar” is always task-dependent.
Section 16.2.3

Why not just train a classifier?

One simple baseline is to train with classification loss, then reuse the penultimate layer as an embedding. The reading describes an O(NC) classification setup for N examples and C classes; actual training cost also depends on the network and optimization.

It is scalable and convenient with class labels. But being on the correct side of a decision boundary does not guarantee that same-class points cluster tightly or that different classes are well separated by distance. Ranking losses target that geometry directly.

03 / Section 16.2.4.1

Siamese: compare a pair.

Picture a face-verification system. Feed in two photos and ask whether their identities match. Both photos pass through the same encoder with shared parameters.

Embedding eⱼ

Two applications. One shared set of weights θ.

The loss compares embeddings. Gradients from both branches update the same θ. This is not two independently trained networks.

Lpair = y d² + (1 − y) max(0, m − d)²

Here y = 1 means similar, y = 0 means dissimilar, and d = ‖êᵢ − êⱼ‖₂ is ordinary Euclidean distance. m > 0 is a margin. This is the standard contrastive-loss convention.

Contrastive loss calculator

Positive pairs are pulled together. Negative pairs are penalized only while d < m; once safely apart, their loss is zero. They are not pushed apart forever.

Why negatives matter: pulling only positive pairs together permits a useless collapse: every input maps to the same point. Negative-pair terms penalize that collapsed solution. Enumerating all pairs costs O(N²) pair terms.
04 / Section 16.2.4.2

Triplet: compare relationships.

Anchor = your reference photo. Positive = another photo of that person. Negative = a different person. The network learns a relative ordering around the anchor.

Three inputs, the same encoder

a → f(·; θ) → êₐ
p → f(·; θ) → êₚ
n → f(·; θ) → êₙ

q₊ = ‖êₐ − êₚ‖₂²
q₋ = ‖êₐ − êₙ‖₂²
Ltriplet = max(0, q₊ − q₋ + m)

Zero loss requires q₋ ≥ q₊ + m.

If the negative is merely farther away, the margin may still be violated. Unlike the pairwise objective, triplet loss directly compares positive and negative distances in the same expression.

Feel the margin

These are abstract distance inputs, not a simulated training process. Each squared distance is within the unit-vector range [0,4].

01

A violated triplet

q₊ = 0.4, q₋ = 0.6, m = 0.5.

L = max(0, 0.4 − 0.6 + 0.5) = 0.3

Negative is farther, but not by enough.

02

A satisfied triplet

q₊ = 0.4, q₋ = 1.2, m = 0.5.

L = max(0, 0.4 − 1.2 + 0.5) = 0

This triplet contributes no active hinge penalty.

03

The practical catch

All possible triples give O(N³) terms. Use minibatches containing valid positive and negative examples for each chosen anchor.

Zero loss on one triplet does not mean the model is fully trained. Triplet loss also does not force every positive distance to zero.

05 / Section 16.2.4.3

One positive. Many alternatives.

A triplet compares one negative at a time. N-pairs provides a richer signal: choose the positive among multiple candidates using their embedding similarities.

s₊ = êₐᵀêₚ,   sₖ⁻ = êₐᵀêₙₖ
P₊ = exp(s₊) / [exp(s₊) + ∑ₖ exp(sₖ⁻)]
LN-pairs = −log P₊
= log[1 + ∑ₖ exp(sₖ⁻ − s₊)]

The denominator contains one positive and N − 1 negatives; N here counts candidates, not the dataset size.

Increasing positive similarity lowers the loss. Increasing any negative similarity raises it. This resembles softmax classification over candidates, rather than over permanent class identities.

Add more competitors

For clarity, this calculator assigns the same similarity to each negative. Temperature τ = 1; natural logarithms.

Connections to remember: with one negative (N = 2), L = log(1 + exp(s₋ − s₊)), a smooth logistic loss. A similarity-based triplet hinge uses max(0, s₋ − s₊ + margin). N-pairs has the same form as InfoNCE; temperature-scaled variants divide similarities by τ, as in NT-Xent. For unit vectors, a squared-distance triplet equals max(0, 2(s₋ − s₊) + m), so switching conventions rescales the margin and loss.

Distance notation: read before calculating

The book’s Eq. 16.7 defines a squared Euclidean dissimilarity, while Eqs. 16.10–16.11 write d² in their losses. To make every calculator unambiguous, this site uses d = ordinary Euclidean distance and q = d², following the common contrastive and triplet conventions. If you literally substitute Eq. 16.7 as d into a later d², you obtain a fourth power; check the intended convention rather than silently interchanging the two.

For KNN, Mahalanobis distance is dM(x,x′) = √[(x−x′)ᵀM(x−x′)], with positive-definite M. M = I gives Euclidean distance. Detailed linear metric-learning methods in 16.2.1 are outside the assigned scope.

07 / Active recall

Explain it without looking.

Say your answer out loud, then open the explanation. These are study prompts, not answers to your course assessment.

Why is raw pixel distance often a poor face metric?

Lighting and pose can change many pixels without changing identity. A task-relevant embedding should preserve identity while reducing sensitivity to those variations.

What makes a network Siamese?

The two branches apply the same encoder with tied parameters. They do not merely share an architecture; the actual parameter values are shared and updated together.

How does a negative pair’s loss change once d ≥ m?

Its contrastive loss is zero. The margin term stops penalizing that pair; it does not require infinitely large separation.

q₊ = 0.5, q₋ = 0.7, m = 0.4: is the triplet satisfied?

No. L = max(0, 0.5 − 0.7 + 0.4) = 0.2. Zero loss requires q₋ ≥ 0.9. Merely having the negative farther than the positive is insufficient.

Can every example map to the same embedding?

That would satisfy positive-only attraction. But contrastive negatives incur m² at zero distance, and a collapsed triplet incurs m. These objectives penalize collapse; this is not a guarantee that optimization always succeeds.

Why can classification accuracy hide poor embeddings?

A classifier needs to put examples on the correct side of a decision boundary. Its hidden layer is not explicitly required to make within-class distances small or between-class distances large.

What advantage does N-pairs offer over a triplet?

It compares the positive against multiple negatives simultaneously, with a smooth softmax-style objective. Each additional competing negative contributes to the denominator.

How can a gallery recognize a new person later?

Reject weak matches, obtain the new identity label, and add labeled examples to the gallery. Future inputs can match them using the encoder. The quality of rejection depends on the learned metric and calibrated threshold.
